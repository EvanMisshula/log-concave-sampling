You cannot have large fluctuations in a function without large gradients.

Yesterday, I attended the first lecture of our summer course on
optimal transport (and log-concave-sampling), a field that explores
how to move probability distributions efficiently, with applications
in machine learning, physics, and beyond. The focus was on the
Poincaré inequality, a cornerstone of functional analysis that
connects a function’s variance to the energy of its gradients. This
lecture didn’t just reteach familiar notes—it brought the mathematics
to life.

Yet the lecture offered fresh insight. Our professor introduced the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup, a mathematical tool that models how
functions evolve toward equilibrium, much like heat diffusing through
a medium. We explored its properties in the simplest setting: a
quadratic potential with linear drift.

Yet the lecture did not merely repeat the derivations of the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup and its infinitesimal generator
. Instead, the lecture invited us to wrestle with four foundational
inequalities in their most tractable setting: a quadratic potential
and a linear drift.

Looking ahead

We only proved the Poincaré inequality today, but the groundwork is
laid for exploring Langevin–Smoluchowski dynamics, which apply these
ideas to problems like sampling in high-dimensional spaces. Future
lectures promise to deepen our understanding of these connections.
