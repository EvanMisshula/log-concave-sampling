You cannot have large fluctuations in a function without large gradients.

Yesterday, I attended the first lecture of our summer course on optimal transport (and log-concave-sampling), a field that explores how to move probability distributions efficiently, with applications in machine learning, physics, and beyond. The focus was on the Poincaré inequality, a cornerstone of functional analysis that connects a function’s variance to the energy of its gradients. This lecture didn’t just reteach familiar notes—it brought the mathematics to life.

Today we had our first lecture of the summer. I must have read both 6 and 6a countless times before class, along with the paper they were based on. Here is the link:

notes on the Poincaré Inequality

Yet the lecture offered fresh insight. Our professor introduced the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup, a mathematical tool that models how functions evolve toward equilibrium, much like heat diffusing through a medium. We explored its properties in the simplest setting: a quadratic potential with linear drift.

Yet the lecture did not merely repeat the derivations of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup and its infinitesimal generator \(L\). Instead, the lecture invited us to wrestle with four foundational inequalities in their most tractable setting: a quadratic potential and a linear drift.

After some formal algebraic manipulations, our professor introduced us to the functional analogue of covariance, the Dirichlet form:

\[\begin{equation} \mathcal{E}(f,g) \triangleq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} \langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle \, d\gamma \end{equation}\]

and its self-interaction, the Dirichlet energy:

\[\begin{equation} \mathcal{E}(f,f) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} \| \nabla f \|^{2} \, d\gamma \end{equation}\]

We were reminded that the OU semigroup $ P_t $ satisfies:

\[\begin{equation} \nabla (P_t f)(x) = e^{-t} P_t(\nabla f)(x) \end{equation}\]

and by the commutation property, this extends to norms:

\[\begin{equation} \| \nabla (P_t f)(x) \| \leq e^{-t} P_t(\| \nabla f \|)(x) \end{equation}\]

Returning to the standard Gaussian measure \(\gamma\) on $ \mathbb{R}^n $, we define the variance:

\[\begin{equation} \text{Var}_{\gamma}(f) \triangleq \int f^2 \, d\gamma - \left( \int f \, d\gamma \right)^2 \end{equation}\]

The key ideas used in the proof (top of p.5 in the notes) are:

  • The ability to commute \(\nabla\) and \(P_t\), as shown above.
  • The invariance of \(\gamma\) under the semigroup $ (P_t)_{t \geq 0} $.

So we prove: energy bounds variance. A famous inequality (Poincaré) now has both analytic and physical meaning.

Even more astounding: from this, a cascade of seemingly unrelated inequalities and information-theoretic identities emerge. Among them:

  1. Logarithmic Sobolev inequality, which controls entropy:
\[\begin{equation} \text{Ent}_{\gamma}(f) \leq \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{\| \nabla f \|^2}{f} \, d\gamma \end{equation}\]
  1. Talagrand inequality, which bounds Wasserstein-2 distance in terms of the Kullback–Leibler divergence:
\[\begin{equation} W_2(\mu, \gamma)^2 \leq 2 \cdot H(\mu \Vert \gamma) \end{equation}\]

We also encountered critical identities:

  • Fisher information:
\[\begin{equation} I_{\gamma}(f) \triangleq \int \frac{\| \nabla f \|^2}{f} \, d\gamma \end{equation}\]
  • De Bruijn identity, relating the rate of entropy dissipation to Fisher information:
\[\begin{equation} \frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}[f_t] = - I_{\gamma}(P_t f) \end{equation}\]

Looking ahead

We only proved the Poincaré inequality today, but the groundwork is laid for exploring Langevin–Smoluchowski dynamics, which apply these ideas to problems like sampling in high-dimensional spaces. Future lectures promise to deepen our understanding of these connections.

A good proof should teach you something. —Ioannis Karatzas

Yesterday, we learned not just a proof, but another way of seeing mathematics as a beautiful, interconnected system. I invite readers to explore the notes and share their thoughts on these elegant inequalities.
