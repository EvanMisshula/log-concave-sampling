\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = \frac{d}{dt} \int h(P_{t} f ) d \gamma
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = \int h’(P_{t}f)L(P_{t}f) d \gamma
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = \int \log{(P_{t}f)}L(P_{t}f) d \gamma
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = - \int \nabla P_{t}f \cdot \nabla (\log{(P_{t}f)}) d \gamma
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = - \int \frac{|\nabla (P_{t}f)|^2}{P_{t}f} d \gamma
\end{equation}

and finally:

\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E}^{f}(t) = I_{\gamma} (P_{t}f)
\end{equation}

What is the Fisher information in this context and what does it mean?

Setting: Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) Process

The OU process $ X_t $ is a continuous-time Gaussian Markov
process satisfying the SDE:

\begin{equation}
dX_t = -\theta X_t \, dt + \sigma \, dB_t
\end{equation}

Where:

  • $ \theta > 0 $: rate of mean reversion
  • $ \sigma > 0 $: diffusion coefficient
  • $ B_t $: standard Brownian motion

The stationary distribution is:

\begin{equation}
X_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\right)
\quad \text{as } t \to \infty
\end{equation}

Fisher Information of a Function $ f(X_t) $

We ask: What does it mean to take the Fisher Information of a
function of the OU process?

There are multiple interpretations depending on the parameter.

1. Statistical Inference Perspective

We consider the Fisher information of $ f(X_t) $ with respect to a
parameter $ \theta $.

\begin{equation}
I(\theta; f(X_t)) = \mathbb{E}\theta \left[ \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p\theta(f(X_t)) \right)^2 \right]
\end{equation}

Where:

  • $ p_\theta $ is the density of $ f(X_t) $ under parameter $ \theta $
  • This helps assess how well $ f(X_t) $ can estimate $ \theta $

2. Information Geometry / Measure-Theoretic Perspective

Let $ X_t \sim \mu $ and $ f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} $ be
smooth. Define a new measure $ \nu = f_{\sharp} \mu $, the pushforward of
$ \mu $ by $ f $.

Then:

\begin{equation}
I(\nu | \mu) = \int \left| \nabla \log
\left( \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \right) \right|^2 \, d\nu
\end{equation}

This quantifies how much information is preserved after transformation by
$ f $.

3. Functional Fisher Information (Bakry–Émery Form)

Finally, this is how it is used in Wednesday’s lecture. Don’t miss the
delightful story of how this was published in a French journal for
“friends and family only.”

For a positive density $ f $ w.r.t. invariant measure $ \mu $, define:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}(f) := \int \frac{|\nabla f|^2}{f} \, d\mu
\end{equation}

This form is used in log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities. It
measures the ``gradient content’’ of $ f $ under the geometry of the
invariant measure $ \mu $.

Summary Table

Perspective Interpretation of Fisher Information of \( f(X_t) \) Use Case
Statistical Information about parameter \( \theta \) Estimation and inference
Information Geometry Fisher info of the pushforward measure \( f_\#\mu \) Functional inequalities, entropy
Diffusion Semigroup Gradient form \( \mathcal{I}(f) \) Transport, log-Sobolev
