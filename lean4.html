Introduction

Last Wednesday, I gave a lecture on the formal proof assistant Lean 4. I made the case that Lean’s growing math library (mathlib4) represents the largest body of rigorously verified mathematics online. Yet major areas of our field remain entirely missing — including Stochastic Integration, Optimal Stopping, Diffusion Processes, and Optimal Control.

While the recording didn’t work (again — Zoom issues), the presentation slides are available on our background page. I’m proud of the work, but I also believe that Alex Kontorovich makes a deeper and more historically grounded argument in his recent lectures — especially for researchers and graduate students.

In this lecture, Kontorovich traces how our standards of mathematical proof have evolved. Between 1800 and 1900, he explains, the culture of rigor was transformed by figures like Cauchy and Fourier. Rigor didn’t always mean epsilon-delta proofs — that language had to be invented.

Then he draws a striking analogy to Donald Knuth’s invention of \TeX in the 1970s. Knuth created \TeX not for the world, but to typeset his own mathematical papers — which had previously been handed off (often handwritten) to a secretary or typist. Over time — thanks to researchers like Leslie Lamport and others who built macros and tooling on top of TeX — it became not only usable, but easier and cheaper than the old system. This tipping point in usability is sometimes called the Knuth factor.

Kontorovich then brings up another powerful figure: Nico de Bruijn. In the 1960s, de Bruijn pioneered mechanical proof checking with his *Automath system — the earliest prototype of what we now call proof assistants. His vision was that mathematical rigor should be so tight, so precise, that it could be verified by a computer. The obstacle? The de Bruijn factor* — the effort required to write a machine-verifiable proof relative to the informal version — was still

  1. In other words, it was harder, longer, and less readable than the handwritten alternative.

But this is changing.

Kontorovich1 suggests that modern systems like Lean 4 are finally bringing the de Bruijn factor closer to 1 — and that once it dips below 1, we may reach a new cultural tipping point, just as \TeX did decades ago. When formalization becomes easier than informal proof — not for every problem, but for enough of them — that’s when everything changes.

My talk made a slightly different, but related argument: that the Lean 4 community is quietly building a kind of GitHub for mathematics — a shared, public, incrementally constructed repository of rigor. Yes, the Lean Zulip chat has only about 11,000 members today, and mathlib4 has just over 550 contributors. But GitHub also started small. It was once a niche project used by coders — and by 1018, it was big enough to be aquired for \$7.5 billion. Today, the platform is powering over 150 million developers. Formal math might follow a similar arc.

I argue Lean 4 isn’t “easier” yet. But the question is no longer if it will be — only when.
